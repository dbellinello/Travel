Day 1 — Anchorage
🏨 FROM HOTEL: 🚶 4 min · 🚕 3 min → Anchorage Museum
1. Anchorage Museum
📒 Alaska's largest museum — part art gallery, part history archive, part science center, entirely Alaskan. The Alaska Gallery traces the state's arc from ancient Arctic cultures through the Gold Rush and statehood; the Smithsonian Arctic Studies Center brings world-class indigenous collections to the same building. A genuine cultural institution, not a tourist trap.
📖 Wikipedia

🚫 Closed Monday

🚕 12 min → Alaska Native Heritage Center
2. Alaska Native Heritage Center
📒 The world's premier cultural center for Alaska's eleven distinct Native peoples — Athabascans, Yupik, Inupiaq, Alutiiq, and more. A living campus on the shores of Lake Tiulana with full-scale traditional village reconstructions, working Native artisans, and seasonal cultural demonstrations that exist nowhere else. The experience is unhurried, genuine, and quietly extraordinary.
📖 Wikipedia
🎟 Alaska Native Heritage Center admission · alaskanative.net

⏰ ~2 hr

🚕 18 min → Flattop Mountain
3. Flattop Mountain
📒 Alaska's most-climbed peak — a 3,510-foot summit in Chugach State Park with a 360° panorama of Anchorage, Cook Inlet, the Alaska Range, and on clear days the summit of Denali floating 150 miles distant. The 1.5-mile trail from Glen Alps Trailhead is steep but straightforward; the final rock scramble delivers the state's most earned urban viewpoint.
📖 Wikipedia
🏛 Open year-round · Best May–October
🆓 Free · Day-use parking pass required at Glen Alps Trailhead

Day 2 — Turnagain Arm
🏨 FROM HOTEL: 🚕 40 min → Alaska Wildlife Conservation Center
1. Alaska Wildlife Conservation Center
📒 A 200-acre drive-through wildlife sanctuary along Turnagain Arm where rescued grizzly and black bears, wolves, muskox, bison, caribou, lynx, and moose inhabit natural enclosures visible from your car window. The American wood bison herd here is part of the only program that has successfully re-established the species in Alaska — a conservation story playing out in real time.
📖 Wikipedia

2. Portage Glacier
📒 The most-visited attraction in Alaska — a tidewater glacier calving into Portage Lake, reachable only by boat. The M/V Ptarmigan cruise crosses iceberg-dotted water to within a hundred feet of the glacier's blue ice face, where small bergs crack and roll in the wake. The Begich, Boggs Visitor Center interprets the glacier's dramatic retreat in living memory.

Day 3 — Mat-Su Valley
🏨 FROM HOTEL: 🚕 46 min → Knik Glacier Helicopter Tour
1. Knik Glacier Helicopter Tour
📒 A helicopter flight from the Knik River Valley into the heart of one of Alaska's most dramatic glacier systems — Knik Glacier, a 25-mile river of ancient ice that once reached the sea. The flight skims over blue melt pools and crevasse fields before landing directly on the ice surface. Some operators include a guided glacier hike or a dogsled run with Iditarod teams who summer on the ice.
📖 Wikipedia

🚕 52 min → Matanuska Glacier
2. Matanuska Glacier
📒 The largest road-accessible glacier in the United States — 27 miles long, 4 miles wide, and retreating visibly year by year. Guided walks with crampons lead onto the surface past ice towers, blue crevasses, and meltwater channels of a color that exists nowhere else. Unlike every flyover glacier experience, this one puts you on the ice at walking pace, unhurried and close enough to hear it move.

Day 4 — Anchorage Waterfront
🏨 FROM HOTEL: 🚶 5 min · 🚕 2 min → Resolution Park
1. Resolution Park
📒 A tiered waterfront deck at the edge of downtown where Cook Inlet opens before you — on clear days the Alaska Range fills the horizon and Denali floats above the clouds 150 miles away. The Captain Cook monument marks the exact point from which this vast inlet was charted by Europeans in 1778. At dusk the entire inlet turns gold and the mountains go pink.

2. Tony Knowles Coastal Trail
📒 Eleven miles of paved waterfront trail hugging the shore of Knik Arm — Cook Inlet to the left, the Alaska Range reflected across the water, moose browsing wetlands to the right. Start at Resolution Park and walk as far west as the day allows; the first three miles deliver the most spectacular urban wilderness running anywhere in the country, with the city at your back and Alaska ahead.

🚕 9 min → Earthquake Park
3. Earthquake Park
📒 The ground here still bears the scars of the 1964 Good Friday Earthquake — at 9.2 magnitude, the most powerful ever recorded in North America. An entire residential neighborhood slid into Cook Inlet as the land liquefied in four minutes; the buckled, sunken terrain that remains is a geological artifact, a monument to the forces still at work under Alaska's surface, and a deeply strange place to stand.
📖 Wikipedia
🏛 Open year-round
🆓 Free
⏰ ~45 min
📍 5101 Point Woronzof Road · West Anchorage

1. Exit Glacier
📒 The only road-accessible section of Kenai Fjords National Park — an active glacier you can walk up to on foot, with dated marker posts documenting its retreat decade by decade. The easy Outwash Plain path brings you face-to-face with blue-green ice; the steep Harding Icefield trail climbs above the glacier to where one of the planet's largest icefields stretches to every horizon.

2. Kenai Fjords Wildlife Cruise
📒 A four-hour voyage through Resurrection Bay aboard a Major Marine vessel — the richest wildlife corridor on Alaska's Gulf Coast. Orcas, humpback whales, Steller sea lions hauled out on spray-soaked rocks, tufted puffins, bald eagles, and mountain goats on cliffs that plunge straight into the sea. The fjord walls rise hundreds of feet on both sides; every turn opens onto something different.

🍮 Local Tastes
Smoked Wild Salmon
Wild Alaska salmon — king, sockeye, or silver — cured and cold-smoked over alder wood. The defining flavor of the state, sold whole or in vacuum packs at fish markets across Anchorage. Nothing sold under this name anywhere else compares to what leaves here fresh from the source.
📍 New Sagaya City Market · 10th & M Seafoods
Reindeer Sausage
Domestic reindeer raised in Interior Alaska, ground and spiced into sausage served in a bun with onions and mustard from street carts and diners across Anchorage. A genuinely Alaskan fast food that predates the state by decades and has no real equivalent anywhere else in the United States.
📍 Anchorage Saturday Market
King Crab
Red king crab hauled from the Bering Sea — legs the size of baseball bats, sweet and firm meat that the rest of the world gets frozen or at great expense. In Anchorage you can eat it the day it arrives. Served steamed, cracked, with drawn butter. There is no version of this dish that is better than the one available here.
📍 Glacier BrewHouse · Crimson
Akutaq
Alaska Native "Eskimo ice cream" — traditionally whipped fat (historically seal or caribou) folded with wild berries, dried fish, and sometimes sugar. Modern versions use shortening, but the texture and the wild berry flavor are unchanged. Found at the Alaska Native Heritage Center during cultural demonstrations and at Native food events around the city.
📍 Alaska Native Heritage Center

🌎 Alaska in Scale

Everything about Alaska resists normal units of measurement. Matanuska Glacier is larger than the entire city of Anchorage. Knik Glacier, which you land on by helicopter in an afternoon, is 25 miles long — longer than Manhattan. The 1964 earthquake lasted four minutes; in those four minutes it moved more earth than any other seismic event in recorded North American history. The Alaska Railroad, which you board before sunrise on Day 5, was completed in 1923 and has been running the same route ever since, still the only land connection between Anchorage and Seward. Denali, visible from downtown on a clear day, is 150 miles away and 20,310 feet tall — nearly four miles higher than Anchorage's elevation. Alaska is a place where the numbers stop feeling real and the geography starts requiring a different vocabulary altogether.

The bore tide at Turnagain Arm — visible on the Seward Highway drive on Day 2 — is one of only a handful in the world. The incoming tide funnels into the narrowing arm faster than it can spread, creating a wave that rolls for miles at up to 6 feet tall. The local name "Turnagain" comes from Captain Cook, who sailed into the arm in 1778 hoping for a northwest passage and was forced to turn again, twice. The Captain Cook monument at Resolution Park on Day 4 marks the end of that failed search. Two hundred and forty-eight years later, the same mountains he saw are still there.
